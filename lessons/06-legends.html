Lesson 6 · Legends and lore

Winter stories

Thunderbird, Iktomi, Unktehi, Deer Woman and the wrestled ghost

Our stories were told in winter, after the first snow, when the snakes were asleep underground and could not overhear. They were how children learned law, courage, manners and the shape of the world, with a laugh or a shiver to make the lesson stick.

Reading time about 6 minutes · Ages 12 and up · Part of the Oceti Sakowin archive

Wakinyan, the Thunder Beings

In the west, where the storms come from, live the Wakinyan: great winged beings whose eyes flash lightning and whose wingbeats are thunder. They are the enemies of the water monsters and the cleansers of the world; a spring storm is the Thunder Beings coming home. A person who dreams of them becomes a heyoka, a sacred clown who must do everything backward: ride facing the tail, shiver in July, say yes for no. The heyoka's foolishness is medicine. When everyone is too serious or too proud, the backward one shows them the other side.

Unktehi, the water monsters

Under the rivers and lakes live the Unktehi, enormous horned serpents whose thrashing causes floods. Long ago the Thunder Beings fought them, and the bones of the losers are still in the ground: the great fossils of the Badlands, which the Lakota knew and named centuries before any geologist arrived. The story holds a real memory of a drowned, ancient world, and it taught every child to respect the Missouri in flood.

Iktomi, the trickster

Iktomi is the spider, and he is the most useful character we have, because he is everything a person should not be: greedy, vain, lazy, boastful, clever in all the wrong ways. In one story he tricks ducks into dancing with their eyes closed so he can wring their necks one by one, then loses the whole feast to a fox while he naps. In another he asks a boulder for its blanket back and is chased for miles. Children howl with laughter at him, and in laughing they learn the rules he breaks. He also, by accident, invents useful things: it was Iktomi who wove the first dream catcher, in the Lakota telling, and who gave the people the knowledge of how to name things.

Deer Woman

A beautiful stranger appears at a dance and chooses a young man who has been careless with women. He follows her away from the fire. Only when she walks ahead of him does he see her hooves beneath the hem of her dress. Those who follow Deer Woman are found wandering, or not found at all. The story is a warning about appetite and respect, and women tell it to young men as often as men tell it to young women.

The man who wrestled a ghost

A warrior traveling alone camps in an old grove and hears a voice invite him to wrestle. He accepts, and the figure that steps out is a wanagi, a ghost, with bare bones for hands. They wrestle all night. Each time the fire burns low the ghost grows stronger; each time the man kicks the embers into flame the ghost weakens. At dawn the ghost gives up and, in exchange for being released, promises the warrior success in the raid he is riding to. He goes on and comes home with horses and honor. The lesson is older than the story: keep your fire up when the night is long, and even the dead will respect you.

Why we still tell them

Every one of these stories carries a rule that no one ever has to state. Do not be Iktomi. Do not follow Deer Woman. Keep your fire. Thank the Thunder Beings for the rain. Children who grow up on them grow up knowing what the nation expects of them, long before anyone writes it down. That is why the game on this site is built out of stories: it is the newest winter telling.

Did you know

  • Lakota people were describing the fossil bones of the Badlands as the remains of the Unktehi long before the first paleontologists dug there in the 1840s.
  • The dream catcher, now sold everywhere, began as an Ojibwe and Lakota cradleboard charm; in the Lakota story Iktomi weaves it while teaching an elder about the cycle of life.
  • A heyoka is one of the most respected and most feared roles in Lakota society because a heyoka cannot lie.

Questions for the circle

For classrooms, families and anyone reading alone. There are no answer keys; the talking is the lesson.

  1. Pick one Iktomi story and write down the rule it teaches without ever saying the rule.
  2. Why would a people make their most sacred dreamers behave like clowns?
  3. What is your family's "keep your fire up" story: the one told to make children brave?
