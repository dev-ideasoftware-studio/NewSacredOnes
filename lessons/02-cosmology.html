Lesson 2 · Cosmology

How the world was made

Wakan Tanka, Inyan and Turtle Island

Our creation stories are not about a distant god building a machine. They are about relationship: how rock, earth, sky, sun and people came to be relatives, and why that family tie is the law we live under.

Reading time about 6 minutes · Ages 12 and up · Part of the Oceti Sakowin archive

The Great Mystery

Wakan Tanka is usually translated "Great Spirit," but the elders prefer "Great Mystery." It is not one being on a throne. It is the whole of sacred power moving through everything that exists. Each thing carries a share of it, called nagila, a spark of the divine. A stone has it. A cottonwood has it. So do you. That is why, at the end of every prayer, we say Mitakuye Oyasin: all my relatives.

The first creation: Inyan the Rock

In the beginning there was only Han, the dark, and Inyan, the Rock, who was soft and had all power inside himself. Inyan was alone, and wanted a companion. So he opened his veins and let his blood flow out of him. The blood became Maka, the Earth, and the water that covers her. What was left of Inyan hardened; that is why rock is hard today, and why the oldest stones are the oldest grandfathers. Maka complained of the cold and the dark, so Skan, the Sky, was made, and then Wi, the Sun, to give warmth and light. From these four came everything else: the moon, the wind, the thunder beings, the animals and finally the human beings, who were the youngest and so had the most to learn.

The second creation: Turtle Island

There is a second story, told across many nations of the plains and the woodlands. The world grew wicked, and the Creator cleansed it with a flood. Only Kangi, the Crow, was left, circling with nowhere to land. The Creator took animals from his pipe bag and asked each to dive for a piece of the old earth. Loon dove and came up empty. Otter dove and drowned. Beaver tried and failed. Then little Turtle, Keya, went down for so long everyone gave her up, and surfaced with mud under her claws. The Creator spread that mud over her shell and sang, and it grew into the land we stand on. That is why North America is Turtle Island, and why the turtle is a sign of patience and long life on cradleboards and in beadwork.

The pipe as a map of the universe

When we fill the sacred pipe we touch the stem to the four directions, then to the sky and the earth, and then we hold it to our own heart: the seventh direction. The pipe bowl is red stone from the quarry at Pipestone, which the stories say is the blood of our ancestors made solid. The stem is wood, the living world. Joined, they are a small model of everything: stone and life, earth and sky, one circle.

Why cosmology is practical

If a buffalo is a relative, you do not waste it. If the river is a relative, you do not poison it. If the land was given to be lived on with permission from the Creator, it cannot be sold, because no one owns a grandmother. Our ancestors' refusal to sell the Black Hills, even for a settlement now worth more than a billion dollars, comes directly from this lesson.

Did you know

  • The Bighorn Medicine Wheel has 28 spokes, the number of days in a lunar month and of poles in a ceremonial lodge.
  • The red stone used for pipe bowls, catlinite, is quarried by Native people only, under a treaty still honored at Pipestone National Monument in Minnesota.
  • The Lakota word for the Milky Way, Wanagi Tacanku, means the Spirit Road: the path the dead walk to the next world.

Talk about it

Questions for the circle

For classrooms, families and anyone reading alone. There are no answer keys; the talking is the lesson.

  1. The people were made last in the first creation. What does that order say about how we should treat the older relatives: rock, water, sun?
  2. Turtle succeeded where stronger animals failed. What was her strength?
  3. Name one thing in your life you treat as a relative rather than a resource.
