Lesson 1 · Who we are

Identity and naming

The Seven Council Fires

Every people begins with a name they call themselves. Ours is Oceti Sakowin, the Seven Council Fires. This lesson is about that name, the name we were given by others, and why the difference still matters on the Fort Peck Reservation today.

Reading time about 6 minutes · Ages 12 and up · Part of the Oceti Sakowin archive

The name we were given

Most of the world knows us as "Sioux." The word is a leftover from the fur trade: French traders shortened an Ojibwe word, Nadouessioux, that meant something like "little snakes" or "speakers of a foreign tongue." It was never ours. It stuck because it was printed on treaties, maps and government rolls for two hundred years, and because the people who wrote those documents did not ask.

The name we call ourselves

Oceti Sakowin means Seven Council Fires. Long before the reservations, seven related peoples would gather in a great camp. Each band carried coals from its own fire, and from those coals a single council fire was lit at the center. The fire was the government: decisions were made around it, by consensus, with the women's lodges and the men's societies each holding a voice. When the camp broke, the coals went home again. Seven fires, one nation.

Three dialects, one language

The seven fires speak one language in three dialects. The four eastern fires are Dakota: Mdewakanton (Dwellers by the Sacred Lake), Wahpekute (Shooters Among the Leaves), Wahpeton (Dwellers Among the Leaves) and Sisseton (Dwellers of the Fish Ground). The two middle fires, Yankton and Yanktonai, are Nakota (the "Dwellers at the End" and "Little Dwellers at the End"). The western fire, the Teton or Lakota, grew so large on the plains that it has seven bands of its own, including the Oglala, Hunkpapa, Sicangu and Minneconjou. The names tell you where people camped and what they ate; they are a map written in words.

Fort Peck in the circle

Our reservation is shared by Assiniboine (Nakoda) and Sioux people, and the Sioux here are mostly Dakota and Yanktonai, with Hunkpapa families who came north with Sitting Bull's people after 1881. When you hear a Fort Peck elder say Dakota instead of Lakota, you are hearing the eastern fire. Both words mean the same thing: friend, ally. That is what the nation named itself: the allies.

Why a name is worth a lesson

Taking a people's name is the first step in taking everything else, because a people who are only described by outsiders have no voice in the description. Using Oceti Sakowin, Dakota, Nakota and Lakota is not a courtesy. It is the small daily act of a nation describing itself. We use "Sioux" on this site only where a treaty, a law or a place name requires it.

Did you know

  • The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty calls us "the different bands of the Sioux Nation of Indians"; the people who signed it called themselves Lakota and Dakota.
  • The Fort Peck Reservation covers more than two million acres along the Missouri River in northeast Montana.
  • Dakota was one of the first Indigenous languages in North America to have a dictionary, published by missionaries in 1852, built from the words of Dakota speakers.

Questions for the circle

For classrooms, families and anyone reading alone. There are no answer keys; the talking is the lesson.

  1. What is the difference between a name you choose and a name you are given? Think of one from your own life.
  2. Find Fort Peck on the map above. Which of the seven fires lived nearest to it before the reservations?
  3. Why might a trading partner's nickname end up on a government treaty?
