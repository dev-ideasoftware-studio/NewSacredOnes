Lesson 7 · Indigenous ingenuity

Engineers of the open plains

Tipi, travois, pemmican, winter counts and sign language

Long before "sustainable" was a word, the Oceti Sakowin ran a mobile, zero-waste civilization across a thousand miles of grass. This is the chapter our technology students read first, because engineering is a tradition here.

Reading time about 6 minutes · Ages 12 and up · Part of the Oceti Sakowin archive

The tipi: a home you can move in an hour

A tipi is not a cone. It is a tilted cone, steeper at the back, so it leans into the prevailing west wind and the smoke hole sits off-center over the fire. Twelve to twenty-four lodgepole pines, a cover of fifteen or more buffalo hides sewn by a women's guild, an inner liner that creates a chimney draft and keeps the floor warm, and adjustable smoke flaps worked from outside by two poles. It is cool in July, warm at thirty below, stands in a prairie gale, and two women can raise it in under an hour. Every tipi belonged to the woman who made it.

The travois and the horse revolution

Before horses, dogs dragged the household on an A-frame of two poles, the travois, carrying up to fifty pounds. When horses arrived in the early 1700s the Lakota re-engineered everything in two generations: longer poles, bigger tipis, heavier loads, and a people who could follow the herds three hundred miles in a season. The pole-and-saddle hitch they designed is still used by outfitters.

Pemmican: the original energy bar

Dried lean buffalo meat pounded to powder, mixed half and half with rendered fat and sometimes dried chokecherries, packed into rawhide bags sealed with tallow. Pemmican keeps for years without refrigeration, delivers more calories per pound than almost any modern ration, and fueled the entire northern fur trade. Arctic explorers carried it into the twentieth century. Today Native food companies are bringing it back.

Rawhide, bone and sinew: nothing wasted

From one buffalo: meat and marrow for food; hide for lodge covers, robes, moccasins, shields and parfleche storage boxes painted with geometric maps; sinew for thread and bowstrings; bones for tools, sleds and dice; horns for spoons and cups; the stomach for a cooking pot, boiling water with hot stones; the bladder for a canteen; the skull for ceremony; dung for fuel on a treeless prairie. The animal that fed the people also housed, clothed, armed and warmed them. That is the standard a circular economy is still reaching for.

Winter counts: history in pictures

Each band kept a waniyetu wowapi, a winter count: a buffalo hide on which one picture was painted for each year, chosen by the band to stand for the year. Lone Dog's count runs from 1800 to 1871 and records a meteor shower in 1833 that astronomers can date to the night. The keeper memorized the story behind every picture. It is a calendar, an archive and a mnemonic system in one object, and it is why Lakota oral history can be checked against the sky.

Sign language and the stars

The plains held dozens of languages, so the nations built a shared one with their hands: Plains Indian Sign Language, fluent across a region larger than Europe, used for trade, diplomacy and storytelling. Our ancestors also mapped the sky in detail: the same star patterns were mirrored on the ground in the Black Hills, so that the people's yearly movement through the Hills followed the sun through the constellations. Astronomy, cartography and ceremony were one subject.

From travois to GPU

A tipi liner is an airflow system. A winter count is a database. Pemmican is food science. Sign language is a protocol. Our ancestors looked at a hard environment and designed their way through it with what they had. The youth training at our data center is the same instinct with new material. We do not teach technology as something foreign to this culture; it is the culture, continued.

Did you know

  • The 1833 Leonid meteor storm appears in nearly every known Lakota winter count as "the winter the stars fell."
  • A large tipi cover took fifteen to eighteen buffalo hides and the work of a dozen women over several days of a sewing feast.
  • Plains Indian Sign Language was documented in a 1930 government film in which leaders of a dozen nations conversed fluently without a word of spoken English.

Questions for the circle

For classrooms, families and anyone reading alone. There are no answer keys; the talking is the lesson.

  1. List everything one buffalo provided. Now list what one car provides, and what is left over when it dies.
  2. Winter counts chose one event per year. What single picture would you paint for this year?
  3. Where in your own house is there a design as clever as a tipi liner?
