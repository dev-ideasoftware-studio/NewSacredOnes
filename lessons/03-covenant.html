Lesson 3 · The sacred covenant

The gift of the pipe

White Buffalo Calf Woman and the seven rites

Nineteen generations ago, when the people were hungry and quarreling, a woman came walking across the prairie carrying a bundle. What she brought still shapes every ceremony we hold.

Reading time about 6 minutes · Ages 12 and up · Part of the Oceti Sakowin archive

Two scouts on the hill

The story begins with two young men sent out to find buffalo in a lean year. On a hill they saw a figure coming toward them: a woman in white buckskin, so beautiful that one of the scouts was filled with greed and reached for her. A cloud covered them, and when it lifted he was a pile of bones with snakes crawling through them. The other scout had lowered his eyes. To him she said: go back and tell your people to build a great lodge, for I am bringing them something.

The bundle

She came into the camp walking sunwise around the lodge and laid down a bundle. Inside was the Chanunpa, the sacred pipe: a bowl of red stone carved with a buffalo calf, a wooden stem hung with twelve eagle feathers. She taught the people how to fill it, how to hold it, and how to pray with it, and she told them that as long as they honored the pipe they would live. Then she walked out of the camp, rolled over on the ground four times, and rose each time as a buffalo calf of a different color: black, brown, red and finally white. Then she was gone, and the buffalo came.

The seven rites

With the pipe came seven ceremonies, and they still order a life from birth to death. Keeping of the soul holds the spirit of someone who has died close to the family for a year before it is released. Inipi, the sweat lodge, is purification: heated stones, water, steam and prayer inside a low dome that is the womb of the earth. Hanbleceya, crying for a vision, sends a person alone to a high place to fast and pray for direction. Wiwanyang Wacipi, the Sun Dance, is the great summer ceremony of renewal in which dancers sacrifice for the people. Hunkapi, making relatives, binds two people as kin through the pipe. Isnati Awicalowanpi honors a young woman at her first moon, when her power to give life begins. Tapa Wankayeyapi, throwing the ball, is a game in which a girl tosses a buffalo-hide ball to the four directions and whoever catches it receives a blessing.

The promise and the white calf

White Buffalo Calf Woman promised to return in a time of trouble to restore harmony. Each time a white buffalo calf is born, people from many nations travel to see it, because it is read as a sign that the promise is still alive. The sacred bundle she left is still kept by a family of the Cheyenne River Sioux; the keeper today is the nineteenth generation.

What the covenant asks

A covenant is a two-way promise. The woman's side was the pipe and the buffalo. The people's side was to live in a sacred manner: to share, to tell the truth, to care for the old and the young, to treat the earth as a mother. Every Sacred Ones project is an attempt to keep our side, with the tools of this century.

Did you know

  • The chance of a white (non-albino) buffalo calf is estimated at about one in ten million births.
  • The 1883 federal "Code of Indian Offenses" made the Sun Dance a crime; it was not legal again until 1978, when the American Indian Religious Freedom Act passed.
  • The sacred pipe bundle has been kept at Green Grass, on the Cheyenne River Reservation, for generations.

Talk about it

Questions for the circle

For classrooms, families and anyone reading alone. There are no answer keys; the talking is the lesson.

  1. One scout looked with greed and one with respect. What is the difference between those two ways of looking?
  2. Why do you think the Sun Dance was outlawed for almost a century? What was the government afraid of?
  3. Which of the seven rites marks a stage of life you have been through? How does your own community mark it?
