Lesson 4 · Leaders

Guardians of the people

Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, Red Cloud, Black Elk

A Lakota leader was not a king. He was followed only as long as he fed, protected and spoke truthfully for the people, and he could be left at any time. These four were followed for life.

Reading time about 6 minutes · Ages 12 and up · Part of the Oceti Sakowin archive

Sitting Bull, Tatanka Iyotake (c. 1831 to 1890)

A Hunkpapa holy man and war leader, Sitting Bull was known first for generosity: he gave away horses and meat until he owned almost nothing. Before the Little Bighorn he danced the Sun Dance and saw soldiers falling into camp upside down, and the vision came true. He led his people to Canada rather than surrender, came back in 1881 when the buffalo were gone, and spent his last years at Standing Rock defending the land against allotment. He was killed by reservation police sent to arrest him in December 1890. Some of his followers, fleeing south, were at Wounded Knee two weeks later. Hunkpapa families who came back from Canada with him settled at Fort Peck, and their descendants are here today.

Crazy Horse, Tasunke Witko (c. 1840 to 1877)

An Oglala war leader who never sat for a photograph, Crazy Horse was quiet, strange and brave past understanding. As a young man he had a vision of a rider who could not be hit by bullets as long as he took nothing for himself; he lived that way, charging first and leaving the spoils to others. He defeated Crook at the Rosebud and led the charge that broke Custer's line eight days later. He surrendered in 1877 to save his starving people and was bayoneted at Fort Robinson while being led to a cell. He is buried in a place only his family knew. The mountain carving in the Black Hills bears his name but not, his relatives say, his face: no one knows it.

Red Cloud, Mahpiya Luta (1822 to 1909)

Red Cloud did what no other Native leader did: he fought the United States to a standstill and made it sign. From 1866 to 1868 his Oglala, Minneconjou and Cheyenne allies closed the Bozeman Trail and besieged its forts until the army abandoned them and burned them. The 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty that followed promised the Black Hills to the Lakota forever. Red Cloud then spent forty years as a negotiator and a thorn, traveling to Washington, speaking to crowds in New York, and refusing to pretend the broken promises were anything else. "They made us many promises," he said near the end, "more than I can remember, but they never kept but one; they promised to take our land, and they took it."

Black Elk, Hehaka Sapa (1863 to 1950)

At nine years old Black Elk lay sick for twelve days and traveled, in a vision, to the center of the world, where the six grandfathers showed him the sacred hoop of his people and a flowering tree. He was a cousin of Crazy Horse, fought at the Little Bighorn as a boy, toured Europe with Buffalo Bill, and survived Wounded Knee. In old age he told his vision to a poet, and the book Black Elk Speaks carried Lakota thought around the world. He later became a Catholic catechist without ever renouncing the vision, and his grandchildren say he held both all his life.

What they had in common

None of them got rich. All of them put the people before themselves, including the ones who disagreed with them. All four spent their last years on reservations negotiating for food, schools and land with a government that lied to them, and none of them stopped. That is the model of leadership this reservation still measures its leaders against.

Did you know

  • Sitting Bull's name is better translated "Buffalo Bull Who Sits Down," the picture of a bull who will not be moved.
  • Red Cloud's War is the only Indian war in which the United States signed a treaty conceding every demand of the Native side.
  • Black Elk's great vision was received at about the age of nine, during a twelve-day illness in 1872.

Talk about it

Questions for the circle

For classrooms, families and anyone reading alone. There are no answer keys; the talking is the lesson.

  1. Crazy Horse's vision told him he would be safe only if he took nothing for himself. How is that different from how we usually picture a hero?
  2. Red Cloud won his war and still lost the Black Hills. Was he a success or a failure? Argue both sides.
  3. Which of these four would you want as a leader in a crisis today, and why?
