Lesson 5 · Resistance

Wars for the land

Red Cloud's War, the Little Bighorn and Wounded Knee

Three dates: 1868, 1876, 1890. One treaty won by force of arms, one battle won and then punished, one massacre that ended a century of fighting. Together they explain why "sovereignty" is not an abstract word on this reservation.

Reading time about 6 minutes · Ages 12 and up · Part of the Oceti Sakowin archive

1866 to 1868: Red Cloud's War

Gold was found in Montana, and the government cut a road, the Bozeman Trail, straight through the last good buffalo country of the Powder River. Red Cloud warned the commissioners that he would fight, and did. For two years Lakota, Northern Cheyenne and Arapaho fighters made the trail impassable, wiped out an eighty-man detachment under Captain Fetterman, and kept three forts under siege. In 1868 the United States gave up. It abandoned the forts, which the Lakota burned, and signed the Fort Laramie Treaty: the Black Hills and the land west of the Missouri were to be the Great Sioux Reservation, and the Powder River country was "unceded Indian territory" where no white person could settle. It was the high mark of Native military power on the plains.

1874 to 1876: the Black Hills and the Greasy Grass

Six years later Custer led an expedition into the Black Hills, in open violation of the treaty, and announced gold. Miners poured in. The government tried to buy the hills; the Lakota refused. So in the winter of 1875 every Lakota and Cheyenne was ordered onto the reservation by January 31, an impossible deadline in a plains winter, and those who did not come were declared hostile. Three columns marched to round them up. On June 25, 1876, Custer found the largest camp anyone had seen, thousands of people along the Little Bighorn, the river we call the Greasy Grass, and attacked it. Crazy Horse, Gall and the Cheyenne leaders broke his command in less than an hour. Sitting Bull, who had foreseen it, protected the women and children. It was a defense of families in their own camp, not an ambush, and it was total.

The punishment

The victory cost everything. Congress responded by cutting off rations until the Lakota "agreed" to give up the Black Hills, a deal signed by about ten percent of adult men when the treaty required three quarters. The army hunted the bands through the winter. Crazy Horse surrendered in May 1877 and was dead by September. Sitting Bull crossed into Canada. The buffalo, hunted for hides and slaughtered as policy, were gone from the northern plains within a decade. In 1980 the Supreme Court ruled the taking of the Black Hills illegal and awarded compensation; the Sioux nations have refused the money, now more than a billion dollars, because the Hills are not for sale.

1890: Wounded Knee

By 1889 the Great Sioux Reservation had been broken into six small ones and the people were starving. A Paiute prophet far to the west, Wovoka, taught a dance: dance, be peaceful, and the dead relatives and the buffalo would return. The Ghost Dance spread across the Lakota reservations as a prayer of the desperate. Agents panicked and called for troops. Sitting Bull was killed during an arrest on December 15. Big Foot's Minneconjou band, mostly women, children and old people, fled toward Pine Ridge and were intercepted by the Seventh Cavalry, Custer's old regiment, at Wounded Knee Creek. On the morning of December 29, while the men were being disarmed, a shot was fired, and the soldiers opened up with rifles and Hotchkiss guns. Between 250 and 300 Lakota died, most of them unarmed; many were shot down while fleeing through the snow. Twenty soldiers received the Medal of Honor. The bodies were buried in a mass grave on the hill where the Wounded Knee cemetery is today.

What the three dates teach

A treaty held only as long as the other side feared us. A battle won in the open was answered with hunger. A prayer for the return of the dead was answered with artillery. We tell this to our children not to make them bitter but so they understand why we build our own data center, raise our own buffalo and keep our own records. Dependence is the lesson of 1877 and 1890. Sovereignty is the answer.

Did you know

  • The Fetterman fight of December 1866 was, until the Little Bighorn, the army's worst defeat on the plains.
  • The Lakota and Cheyenne camp on the Little Bighorn is estimated at six to eight thousand people, with perhaps fifteen hundred to two thousand fighters.
  • In 1990 Congress passed a resolution expressing "deep regret" for Wounded Knee; the Medals of Honor have never been rescinded.

Talk about it

Questions for the circle

For classrooms, families and anyone reading alone. There are no answer keys; the talking is the lesson.

  1. The 1868 treaty required three quarters of adult men to sign away land. About ten percent did. Is a contract valid if one side was starving? Who decides?
  2. Why have the Sioux nations refused more than a billion dollars for the Black Hills? What would you have to believe to make that choice?
  3. The Ghost Dance was a prayer. Why did it frighten the government so badly?
